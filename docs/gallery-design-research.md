# PART 1: PHYSICAL GALLERY DESIGN

## 1. Spatial design

### 1.1 The white cube: origin

**The text.** Brian O'Doherty published three essays in *Artforum* in 1976: "Notes on the Gallery Space," "The Eye and the Spectator," and "Context as Content." Collected as *Inside the White Cube: The Ideology of the Gallery Space* (Lapis Press, 1986); expanded edition University of California Press, 1999, adding "The Gallery as Gesture" (1986) and an introduction by Thomas McEvilley.

Quotable lines, verbatim:

- "A gallery is constructed along laws as rigorous as those for building a medieval church."
- "The outside world must not come in, so windows are usually sealed off. Walls are painted white. The ceiling becomes the source of light."
- "Some of the sanctity of the church, the formality of the courtroom, the mystique of the experimental laboratory joins with chic design to produce a unique chamber of esthetics."
- "Art exists in a kind of eternity of display, and though there is lots of 'period' (late modern), there is no time."
- "The space offers the thought that while eyes and minds are welcome, space-occupying bodies are not."
- "Once the wall became an esthetic force, it modified anything shown on it."
- "We have now reached a point where we see not the art but the *space* first."

That last pair is the operative design claim: the container is not passive. Wall color, spacing and light are authored content, and the pretence that they are not is the ideology.

**The prehistory, before Barr.** Charlotte Klonk, "The White Wall: On the Prehistory of the 'White Cube'" (*OnCurating*, issue 9), traces it earlier than the usual MoMA story:

- Before white, gallery walls were *Galerierot* (deep gallery red), damask, embroidered silk, patterned fabric in pinkish yellow and light green, with gold-tooled leather trim. Monochrome grey (from around 1888) and dark blue (1895) were transitional. Grey and yellow gave way to white gradually across roughly 1870 to 1940.
- **Vienna Secession building, 1897**, architect Joseph Maria Olbrich: generally cited as the earliest pure white walls in an art space, though initially with gold ornament. By the 1903 to 1904 Secession exhibitions the walls were framed with simple wooden slats, the fields between left white, and pictures hung in a **single row**.
- **Hugo von Tschudi, Nationalgalerie Berlin, 1906**: kept Peter Behrens's design from the Centennial Exhibition of German Art, making it the first museum to show its permanent collection against a white ground.
- **Karl Ernst Osthaus and the Museum Folkwang** (Hagen), with Henry van de Velde, from around 1902.
- **Klimt room, Venice Biennale 1910**: single-row hang on light monochrome walls. **Sonderbund, Cologne 1912**: white walls, sparse dark lines.
- **Ludwig Justi, Kronprinzenpalais, Berlin, from 1919**: Expressionists on pure white, single row, **bottom edges aligned**. Klonk calls this one of the first permanent museum displays fully conforming to modern expectations.
- **El Lissitzky, *Abstract Cabinet*, Provinzialmuseum Hannover, 1927 to 1928**, commissioned by Alexander Dorner: the counter-example, a room with active, kinetic, striped wall surfaces.

**Barr at MoMA.** Alfred H. Barr Jr. saw the Folkwang collection in 1927 and applied it at MoMA from 1929: walls lined in **beige monk's cloth**, described as "the most neutral thing he could get," works in a single row **just below eye level**, generous interstitial space. Codified in *Cubism and Abstract Art* (1936). The cloth became paint and the beige became white by the time MoMA moved to West 53rd Street. Harvard's Fogg and the Wadsworth Atheneum ran comparable installations in the early 1930s; MoMA's publicity is what institutionalised it.

### 1.2 Critiques

**Elena Filipovic, "The Global White Cube"** (*OnCurating* 22): the white cube became "a cipher for institutional officiousness." Its neutrality is a production: the "ideal viewer (white, middle-class) is also constructed: well behaved, solemn, disembodied." Her sharpest formulation: "ideology and form meet, and all without our noticing it." Her historical bomb: the same aesthetic served the Haus der Kunst in Munich in 1937 for the Great German Art Exhibition, which demonstrates the format's "usefulness, efficacy, and versatility" across opposed ideologies.

Her structural critique is about export: biennials claiming to resist Western institutional frameworks reproduce the white cube globally, and she asks whether "a true decentering of traditional notions of modernity" is possible "so long as the Western museum's frame is exported." Manifesta, she argues, accepted the white cube "as a kind of 'international-style' exhibition frame."

Named counter-examples she cites:

- **Gwangju Biennial 2002, *P.A.U.S.E.***, directed by Wan-kyung Sung, where artist collectives built their own pavilions instead of standard white partitions (an AES Group Bedouin tent; a carpet-lined photocopying facility from Kurimanzutto where visitors Xeroxed cheap catalogues).
- **documenta X, 1997**, Catherine David, whose *100 Days / 100 Guests* displaced the object: the work in the room becomes "merely the support and the vector of highly diverse artistic activities."

**Institutional critique.** Daniel Buren, "The Function of the Museum" (1970): "the Museum makes its 'mark,' imposes its 'frame'." Companion text: "The Function of the Studio" (1971). Michael Asher and Hans Haacke are the other canonical names. Andrea Fraser, "From the Critique of Institutions to an Institution of Critique," *Artforum*, September 2005, is the standard retrospective assessment.

**Current.** #MuseumsAreNotNeutral, created August 2017 by La Tanya S. Autry and Mike Murawski; their essay "Museums Are Not Neutral: We Are Stronger Together" appeared in *Panorama* (2019). Related canonical reading: Carol Duncan, *Civilizing Rituals: Inside Public Art Museums* (1995), on the visit as a scripted ritual performance; Tony Bennett, *The Birth of the Museum* (1995), on the museum as an instrument for producing a self-regulating public.

### 1.3 Alternatives to the white cube

**Salon hang.** Paris Salon from 1667; works floor to ceiling, frame to frame; "on the line" (at eye level) was the prized position and a jury decision. Still alive at the **Royal Academy Summer Exhibition** and preserved domestically at **Sir John Soane's Museum**, Lincoln's Inn Fields. The **Barnes Foundation** "ensembles," symmetrical wall groupings mixing paintings with African objects and wrought-iron metalwork, were fixed by Albert Barnes's will and reproduced exactly in the 2012 Tod Williams Billie Tsien building in Philadelphia.

Design property of the salon hang: it removes the aura of the individual object and replaces it with abundance, comparison and a democratic flatness. Nothing gets a wall to itself, so nothing is singled out as sacred.

**Black box.** The technical justification is real (projectors are additive light sources; ambient light washes the image), but subdued lighting has hardened from constraint into norm. The behavioural consequence is documented: visitors glance into a dark room and pass, because moving-image work demands duration and the darkness gives no cue about how much. The Generali Foundation, Vienna, staged *White Cube / Black Box* in 1996 as an exhibition about exactly this split. Practical answer used by most institutions now: **publish the runtime on the label and put a bench inside**.

**Warehouse / raw space.**

- **Dia:Beacon** (2003), a 1929 Nabisco box-printing factory of roughly 300,000 sq ft with over 34,000 sq ft of original skylights, converted under the direction of the artist **Robert Irwin** with OpenOffice, preserving the industrial fabric.
- **Palais de Tokyo**, Paris, by Lacaton &amp; Vassal (2001, extended 2012): the budget spent on square metres rather than finish, concrete and services left raw.
- In Berlin, Hamburger Bahnhof and the KW Institute (a derelict margarine factory).

The raw aesthetic reads as generosity of space and honesty of means, but note it is now a luxury signifier as often as a poverty one.

**Kunsthalle and Kunstverein.** A *Kunsthalle* mounts temporary exhibitions and has **no permanent collection**. In German-speaking Europe, Kunsthallen are frequently operated by a non-profit *Kunstverein*. Kunstverein Hamburg founded 1817; Basler Kunstverein showing art from 1839, its Kunsthalle Basel opening 1872 in a neoclassical building by Johann Jakob Stehlin; Kunsthalle Bern founded 1918 (site of Harald Szeemann's *When Attitudes Become Form*, 1969, and Christo and Jeanne-Claude's wrapping of the building, 1968).

The spatial consequence of having no collection is enormous: no permanent hang, no chronological narrative, no storage-to-display pipeline, so the building is a shell that is rebuilt every ten weeks. That is why Kunsthalle interiors tend to be plain, tall and reconfigurable rather than architecturally authored.

**Exhibition architects as a discipline.** Kuehn Malvezzi, founded Berlin 2001 by Simona Malvezzi, Wilfried Kuehn and Johannes Kuehn: architecture for documenta 11 (2002), the Friedrich Christian Flick Collection at Hamburger Bahnhof, and the Julia Stoschek Collection in Düsseldorf. Wilfried Kuehn coined "curatorial design." Simona Malvezzi's principle: "Architecture shouldn't show. It should create spaces that are functional to the purpose of the exhibition and foster the relation between the artwork and the viewer."

### 1.4 Wall color

- **MoMA** has long used an in-house white, **"Super White" by Janovic**, specified in a **matte flat finish** partly so it does not bounce light off the hardwood floors.
- **Farrow &amp; Ball** supplies custom blends to museums and appeared explicitly as content in MoMA's *Items: Is Fashion Modern?* (2017, curated by Paola Antonelli), which used **Wevet**, **Pitch Black** and **Tanner's Brown** as backdrop swatches.
- Commonly named gallery whites: Farrow &amp; Ball **All White** (untinted, no colorant added, so it has no undertone to fight the work) and Benjamin Moore **Chantilly Lace** (very clean, slightly cool).
- **Any undertone makes the wall visible.** Warm whites flatter varnished oil paint and skin tones; cool whites flatter photography and steel; a true untinted white is the only one that disappears.
- **Finish matters more than hue.** Matte / flat (roughly 2 to 5 percent sheen) kills specular reflection and glare from the track lights. It also scuffs, which is why the convention is to **repaint the whole space between every exhibition**. That repaint cycle, roughly six to ten times a year, is both a real cost and the ritual by which the space is declared blank again.
- The counter-move now in fashion at collection museums is deep saturated wall color for historical hangs, a deliberate return to *Galerierot* and to painted period rooms.

### 1.5 Lighting

**Conservation limits.** The governing international document is **CIE 157:2004, *Control of Damage to Museum Objects by Optical Radiation***.

| Sensitivity | Typical maximum illuminance |
|---|---|
| Very sensitive: works on paper, watercolour, textiles, dyed leather, feathers, photographs | **50 lux** |
| Moderate: oil and tempera paintings, lacquer, wood, furniture, bone, ivory, undyed leather, minerals, modern black and white photography | **200 lux** |
| Insensitive: stone, metal, ceramic, most glass | 300 lux and above |

Practice is to manage **cumulative exposure** (lux multiplied by hours), not just instantaneous level, and to trade brighter light against a shorter show. UV is filtered to a low microwatts-per-lumen threshold. Colour rendering for galleries is specified high, commonly CRI Ra 90 as a floor and 95 or above for demanding work, increasingly with TM-30 fidelity and gamut metrics alongside.

**Track.** The European default is **three-circuit track**. **ERCO** uses an identical track profile for three-circuit and DALI modes, so a gallery can upgrade to addressable control without rewiring; **Zumtobel** is the other standard specification. The toolkit is three fixture types:

- **Spotlight**: narrow, for objects.
- **Floodlight**: wide, for groups.
- **Wallwasher**: for even luminance across a whole wall, which is what makes a white cube read as a continuous plane rather than a series of pools.

For ceilings around 2.8 to 3.0 m, track is surface mounted. Density benchmark: a roughly 400 sq m commercial gallery typically runs 80 to 160 individual track spots plus 20 to 40 ambient and case fixtures.

**Aiming convention: roughly 30 degrees from vertical.** Steeper and the frame casts a shadow on the work and the top of the canvas is hot; shallower and the viewer's own body shadows the piece and the glazing throws glare back.

**Daylight.** Three canonical daylight museums:

- **Kimbell Art Museum**, Fort Worth, Louis Kahn, 1972: cycloid concrete vaults with a continuous slot skylight at the apex and a perforated aluminium reflector below, so no direct sun reaches a surface.
- **The Menil Collection**, Houston, Renzo Piano with Peter Rice, 1987: a layered roof of glass (weather and spectral filtering), open steel trusses, and curved ferrocement **"leaves"** below that intercept sunlight and bounce it indirectly downward while carrying the artificial fixtures. Origin of Piano's floating-roof museum language.
- **Dia:Beacon**: north-light factory sawtooth skylights, and a deliberate decision to run the museum on daylight hours, so the work looks different in February than in July.

The argument for daylight is that it restores time to a space O'Doherty said had none. The argument against is conservation and consistency.

### 1.6 Hang height, floors, ceilings, dimensions

**Hang height.** The dominant convention is a **centre line at 57 inches (144.8 cm)** from finished floor, taken as approximate adult eye height; 58 in and 60 in are common institutional variants, and European practice usually states 145 cm or 150 cm.

Two things follow from it being a *centre*-line rule rather than a top- or bottom-line rule: works of wildly different sizes read as optically aligned, and very tall works are pushed low. Historical alternatives are live options: Justi's **aligned bottom edges** (Kronprinzenpalais, 1919), the salon's aligned nothing, and the shelf or ledge hang.

**Spacing:** a modernist "breathing room" hang uses 60 to 120 cm or more between works; a grouped or grid hang uses 5 to 15 cm.

**Ceilings and floor area.**

- Professional galleries: clear height commonly **3.6 to 6.1 m (12 to 20 ft)**, with 4.3 m (14 ft) a workhorse figure. Pace in Chelsea has a 20 ft room.
- Chelsea main-floor gallery: roughly 2,000 to 3,000 sq ft (190 to 280 sq m).
- Artist-run shopfront: 30 to 80 sq m, ceiling 2.8 to 3.2 m, one or two rooms.
- Wall runs of 6 to 9 m (20 to 30 ft) are what allow a modernist hang at all; below about 4 m of unbroken wall, you are effectively hanging one work per wall.

**Floors.** Polished or sealed **concrete** (Dia:Beacon, nearly all Berlin off-spaces), sanded and sealed pine or oak (MoMA), **parquet** (herringbone or chevron) in historic buildings, **poured resin or epoxy**, sealed screed. Carpet is standard in history and ethnographic museums and almost unheard of in contemporary art.

The acoustic consequence is a design decision, not a side effect: hard floors make every footstep audible, and that audibility is a large part of what produces the reverential hush and the self-consciousness of walking into a quiet gallery alone. If you want people to relax, soften the floor or add soft ceiling absorption.

### 1.7 Circulation and routing

- **Right-turn bias.** Early visitor studies reported 70 to 80 percent of visitors turning right and following the right-hand wall. Later replication work presented through the Visitor Studies Association finds this unreliable outside highly symmetrical plans. Treat it as folklore, not a design rule.
- **Plan types.** *Enfilade* (rooms in a line with aligned doorways) enforces sequence and gives the long axial view, at the cost of forcing people through rooms they do not want. *Loop* around a central hall. *Open plan* with free-standing partitions, which gives curatorial freedom and gives visitors none of the reassurance of knowing where they are.
- **Sightlines.** The single most valuable position in any gallery is the work visible on axis through the entrance door. That is the piece that decides whether a passer-by comes in.
- **Dwell time.** Beverly Serrell, "Paying Attention: The Duration and Allocation of Visitors' Time in Museum Exhibitions," *Curator*, 1997, established the **sweep rate index** (exhibition area divided by average total time, so square metres per minute) and **percentage of diligent visitors**, from a meta-analysis of over a hundred exhibitions. The consistent finding: most visitors are casual rather than thorough, and exhibitions systematically overestimate how long anyone will stand at any element. "Museum fatigue" was named by Benjamin Ives Gilman in 1916 and is still the constraint.

### 1.8 How scale changes everything

The Louvre has roughly 72,700 sq m of exhibition space (about 782,910 sq ft) across 300-plus gallery rooms. The Met's Fifth Avenue building is over 2 million sq ft with around 500,000 sq ft of galleries. A Berlin Projektraum in a *Ladenlokal* is 40 to 60 sq m with a sink and one toilet.

What actually changes:

| | 40-room museum | One-room artist-run space |
|---|---|---|
| Wayfinding | A designed system: floor plans, room numbers, directional signage, an app. Getting lost is the primary failure mode. | You can see the whole show from the door. Wayfinding is the fact of the room. |
| Text | Written by an interpretation department, months in advance, to a house style guide, by people who may never meet the artist. | Written by the artist or a friend, printed the night before. |
| Sequence | A curatorial argument built across rooms, with a beginning and an end. | No sequence available. The space works by concentration: one idea, one body of work, one encounter. |
| Staff | Front of house, security, invigilators, ticketing. The visitor is watched. | One person, who is probably the curator or the artist. The visitor is met. |
| Failure mode | Fatigue, crowding, feeling processed. | Feeling trapped in a small room with the person who made the thing. |
| Repaint | A contractor, scheduled. | The organisers, the weekend before, with a roller from the hardware shop. |

**Design consequence for a small space:** do not imitate museum apparatus. A 50 sq m room with a title wall, an intro panel, section panels and forty labels is a parody. The scale-appropriate move is one printed room sheet, one line of text, and a bench.

## 2. Interpretive and textual design

### 2.1 The tombstone

Standard museum field order, top to bottom:

```
Artist Name
(nationality, birth year – death year)      or  (b. 1978, Leipzig; lives in Berlin)
Title of the Work, in italic
Date
Medium on support
Height × width × depth
Edition or state
Credit line: gift, bequest or purchase fund, year
Accession number
```

Worked example in museum form:

> **Hilma af Klint**
> Swedish, 1862–1944
> *The Ten Largest, No. 7, Adulthood, Group IV*
> 1907
> Tempera on paper, mounted on canvas
> 315 × 235 cm
> Courtesy of the Hilma af Klint Foundation

**Commercial gallery variant.** Drops nationality, credit line and accession number, and frequently drops dimensions. Blue-chip practice is often artist, title, date, medium only, or nothing on the wall at all beyond a discreet number keyed to a room sheet. Removing information from the wall is a deliberate move: it pushes you toward the desk, where the conversation, and the price, happen.

**Dimension conventions.**

- Order is **height × width × depth**, always.
- Metric first in Europe, imperial in parentheses; imperial first in the US.
- Works on paper are given twice, as **image** and **sheet**. "Sight" means the visible area inside a mount or frame.
- Paintings are unframed unless stated; sculpture may be given with or without base, stated.
- Time-based work carries duration instead of or beside size: "HD video, colour, sound, 12 min 34 sec."
- Variable installations use "dimensions variable."

### 2.2 Label typography and physical spec

**Size.** 18 pt is the widely-stated floor for object labels in institutional style guides. UC Santa Barbara library exhibition guidance specifies ID labels at a minimum 18 pt and maximum 20 pt; the University of Florida Smathers Libraries requires a minimum 18 pt in Arial. Accessibility-led practice pushes higher: **24 pt is a common recommended minimum** for label body text, and the **Smithsonian Guidelines for Accessible Exhibition Design** is the standard reference for institutions moving past minimum ADA compliance toward universal design.

Wall panel body text typically runs 30 to 48 pt; section headings 60 to 100 pt; title-wall lettering is specified by **cap height** rather than points, commonly 100 to 250 mm.

**Typeface.** Sans serif dominates wall-mounted text because a large x-height and even stroke weight survive distance, low light and oblique viewing angles. Serif persists in art-historical and decorative-arts museums for tonal reasons. Institutions with commissioned or house faces include Tate (a custom Tate typeface), MoMA (Franklin Gothic lineage, with MoMA Gothic drawn to extend it by Matthew Carter), the Walker Art Center (Walker, Matthew Carter, 1995, with detachable snap-on serifs), and Cooper Hewitt (the open-source Cooper Hewitt typeface by Chester Jenkins). Neue Haas Grotesk, Akzidenz-Grotesk and Founders Grotesk are the generic art-world defaults.

**Measure and leading.** 45 to 65 characters per line for panel text, 50 to 60 for labels. Leading at roughly 120 to 140 percent of type size. **Always ranged left, never justified**, because justification opens rivers at short measures and hurts low-vision readers.

**Physical.**

- Card sizes: A6 (105 × 148 mm), A5 (148 × 210 mm), and the US 4 × 6 in are the common label footprints; a tombstone-only label is often 100 × 150 mm or smaller.
- Substrates: 300 gsm card pinned or double-sided taped; digital print mounted on 3 mm foamboard; print on 3 mm Dibond for durability; direct-applied vinyl for a label with no visible edge at all.
- Lectern and case-top labels are tilted, commonly 30 to 45 degrees, so they can be read standing or seated.

**Placement.**

- Labels sit consistently to one side, usually **lower right**, at a constant offset from the frame edge, commonly 5 to 15 cm. Consistency matters more than which side: the visitor should stop hunting after the first work.
- On a wall of several works, all labels align on a single horizontal baseline.
- Vertical centre for standing readers is usually set around 137 to 145 cm. **Accessibility-led practice drops the label centre to roughly 110 to 120 cm** so a seated visitor can read it without craning, which is one of the cheapest inclusive changes available and costs nothing but a decision.
- Wall text baseline is generally set so the first line's cap height sits around 150 to 165 cm, with the block running down from there.

### 2.3 Wall text and panels

**The tier system** ("layered text" or "hierarchy of text"), with typical word budgets:

| Tier | Purpose | Words |
|---|---|---|
| Title wall | Exhibition title, dates, credits, funders | 10 to 30 |
| Introduction panel | The show's argument in one breath | 100 to 200, with 150 a common cap |
| Section panel | Orients one room or theme | 75 to 150 |
| Object label | Tombstone plus interpretation | 50 to 100 of interpretation |
| Extended label | Deeper reading for those who want it | up to 150 |

**The governing method** is Beverly Serrell's *Exhibit Labels: An Interpretive Approach* (1996; second edition 2015). Its central device is the **"big idea"**: a single declarative sentence with a subject, a verb and a consequence, agreed before any text is written, against which every panel and label is tested. If a label does not serve the big idea, it is cut.

**Reading level.** Institutional targets commonly land at a reading age of nine to twelve years, sometimes stated as Flesch-Kincaid grade 6 to 8. Practical rules that appear in most style guides: sentences capped at 25 words with an average of 15 to 20; active voice; second person allowed; jargon defined at first use or removed; no unexplained art-historical shorthand.

**Tone as an institutional tell.** Museums write in an impersonal institutional third person that carries authority precisely by having no author. Artist-run spaces more often print the artist's own words, a commissioned text by a peer, or nothing. The failure mode in the middle is the press-release register anatomised by Alix Rule and David Levine in "International Art English" (*Triple Canopy*, 2012), a dialect that signals membership and functions, in practice, as a door policy made of words.

### 2.4 Vinyl, signage, handouts, price lists

**Cut vinyl** is die-cut, weeded by hand, transferred with application tape, and leaves the painted wall showing between the letters. That is why it reads as institutional: it looks like part of the building rather than something placed on it. It also has to be scraped off and the wall repainted at deinstall. **Printed vinyl** on a mounted panel is cheaper for dense text and reads as applied rather than integral.

The alternatives are not merely cheaper, they say something different:

- **Hand-painted signwriting**: labour, craft, permanence, a human hand. Reads warm and slightly defiant.
- **Stencil and spray**: workshop, squat, temporary.
- **Laser-printed A4 sheets taped up**: honest, urgent, provisional, and legible as "we spent the money on the artist."
- **A photocopied handout**: transfers the text off the wall and into the visitor's hand, which lets them take it home and lets the walls stay clean.

**Room sheet / checklist.** A4, single sheet or folded, numbered to match small numerals beside each work, held in a wall pocket by the door or stacked on the desk. In artist-run spaces this replaces wall labels entirely: zero installation cost, zero wall damage, revisable up to the last hour, and takeable. **It is the single most useful convention a small space can borrow.**

**The commercial price list.** A4 at the desk, never on the wall. Contents: item number, artist, title, date, medium, dimensions, edition and edition size, and price, typically with currency and often "plus VAT" or "excl. VAT." "**Price on request**" (POR) appears when the gallery wants to control disclosure, price differently for different buyers, or protect an artist's public record.

Why it is a sheet at a desk rather than a label on a wall is the most revealing convention in the whole system: the white cube's claim to be a place of disinterested contemplation cannot survive a price on the wall, so the commerce is moved two metres away into a document you have to ask for. **Asking is the filter.** Art fairs have partially broken this since around 2018 under transparency pressure, but the desk sheet remains the gallery norm.

For a space whose values are anti-gatekeeping, publishing prices openly, or publishing plainly that nothing is for sale, removes a specific and well-documented anxiety: not knowing whether you have walked into a shop.

### 2.5 Accessibility

- **Large print.** RNIB's Clear Print guidance sets a general floor around 12 pt and 14 pt where readers may have sight loss; RNIB **Large Print** is 16 pt minimum, with 18 pt and 24 pt commonly offered as the standard tiers. Rules that come with it: sans serif, ranged left and unjustified, generous leading, **matt paper to avoid glare**, no text over images, and no text in all caps. A large-print exhibition guide, A4, spiral bound so it lies flat, 18 pt minimum, sitting at the entrance, is the cheapest meaningful accessibility measure available to a small space.
- **Contrast.** WCAG 2.1 AA's 4.5:1 for body text is the digital baseline institutions increasingly apply to print and signage. Physical signage practice in the UK additionally uses **light reflectance value (LRV)** difference, with **BS 8300-2:2018 asking for a 30-point LRV difference** between a sign and its background surface. Dark text on a light ground or light on dark; avoid mid-grey on white, which is the most common label failure.
- **Braille and tactile.** US ADA signage rules require **Grade 2 (contracted) braille** on permanent room identification, positioned directly below the corresponding text, with raised characters in a sans serif, non-italic face, and tactile signs mounted so the tactile characters sit within a defined band roughly 48 to 60 inches from the floor. Braille is expensive per unit and slow to produce, so in practice it appears on permanent wayfinding rather than on temporary object labels; the usual substitute for exhibitions is audio.
- **Audio description.** UK practice is led by **VocalEyes** (founded 1998). Standard output now: downloadable audio description of individual works, plus a **described introduction to the space itself** (what the room looks like, where things are, how far the walk is), which is useful to far more people than its target audience.
- **Sign language.** BSL in the UK and DGS in Germany, delivered as filmed tours embedded on the website and on tablets in the gallery, and as scheduled interpreted tours.
- **Sensory-friendly and relaxed openings.** Reduced lighting and sound, capped visitor numbers, a designated quiet room, staff briefed that movement and noise are fine, and a **visual story** published in advance: a photographic walk-through describing exactly what a visitor will encounter, from the door handle onward. **Removing surprise is the actual mechanism.**
- **Bodies.** The most under-rated accessibility measure in a gallery is a **place to sit**. It costs one bench.

## 3. Graphic identity and print

### 3.1 Named institutional identities

| Institution | Designer | Year | What the system does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whitney Museum of American Art | **Experimental Jetset** (Amsterdam) | 2013 | The "responsive W": a single zigzag line that stretches, compresses and re-proportions to fit any format, so the mark is a rule rather than a fixed shape. Set with Neue Haas Grotesk. The most cited flexible identity of its decade. |
| MoMA | Chermayeff &amp; Geismar lineage; **Matthew Carter** for the custom face | wordmark from 1964, MoMA Gothic later | Wordmark in Franklin Gothic No. 2, extended into a proprietary MoMA Gothic so the institution owns its voice at every size. |

| The Metropolitan Museum of Art | **Wolff Olins** | 2016 | The red "THE MET" ligatured wordmark. Notorious for the backlash, widely mocked, later quietly softened. The cautionary tale about rebranding an institution whose authority rests on not appearing to try. |
| Centre Pompidou | **Jean Widmer** / Visuel Design | 1977 | The horizontal-line pictogram system, one of the great signage programmes. |
| Barbican Centre | **North** | from the 1990s onward | Rigorous grid and a strong single-colour discipline across a chaotic brutalist building. |

**The lineage that matters more than any single logo:** the **Werkplaats Typografie** in Arnhem, founded 1998 by **Karel Martens** and **Wigger Bierma**, is the pipeline through which most of European art-world graphic design flows.

Related canonical figures: **Irma Boom** (artist books and the SHV Think Book, 1996, 2,136 pages with no page numbers), **Bruce Mau** (S,M,L,XL with Rem Koolhaas, 1995; Zone Books).

Working studios worth naming: Mevis &amp; Van Deursen, Experimental Jetset, Julia Born, Norm (Zurich), Studio Manuel Raeder (Berlin), Bureau Borsche (Munich), HORT (Berlin), NODE Berlin Oslo, OK-RM and Zak Group (London).

### 3.2 Posters and invitation cards

**Formats.** Europe runs on DIN: A0 841 × 1189 mm, A1 594 × 841, A2 420 × 594, A3 297 × 420. German street advertising uses the *Grossfläche* (18/1) and the city-light poster at roughly 1,185 × 1,750 mm. The Swiss street poster is the **Weltformat, 90.5 × 128 cm**, and the entire Basel poster tradition is built on that proportion: **Armin Hofmann**, **Emil Ruder**, **Josef Müller-Brockmann**, the grid, Akzidenz-Grotesk and later Helvetica, and a discipline of one image and one line of type at a size that works from a moving tram.

**The invitation card.** The European default is an **A6 postcard, 105 × 148 mm**, on 300 to 350 gsm; A5 (148 × 210 mm) for a heavier gesture; 4 × 6 in in the US. Front: one image, or nothing but type. Back: artist name, exhibition title, opening date and time, run dates, opening hours, street address, and a strip of funder logos. In Berlin the back reads more or less: *Eröffnung Freitag, 19 Uhr*, the dates, *Öffnungszeiten Sa–So 14–18 Uhr*, the address, and the Senate culture logo.

The card has two lives. At commercial galleries the physical mailing list is still how the collector base is maintained, so the card is a CRM object. At off-spaces nobody mails anything; the cards sit in a stack by the door and function as a free takeaway, a bookmark, and the only artefact of the show that outlives it.

### 3.3 Catalogues and books

**Publishers and their house character.**

- **Steidl** (Göttingen, Gerhard Steidl): photographic monographs, tri-tone and quadratone printing, matt uncoated stock, cloth binding, the printer supervising the press personally.
- **Verlag der Buchhandlung Walther König** (Cologne): the default publisher and distributor of European exhibition catalogues, and the operator of the bookshops inside a large share of European museums.
- **Roma Publications** (Amsterdam, Roger Willems), out of the Werkplaats orbit: cheap, formally exact, often newsprint or uncoated.
- **Spector Books** (Leipzig, Markus Dressen, Anne König, Jan Wenzel), tied to the HGB Leipzig: the current benchmark for exhibition books as designed objects.
- **Sternberg Press** (Berlin, Caroline Schneider): small-format critical theory and artist books, unadorned.
- **MACK** (London), **JRP|Editions** (Geneva), **Hatje Cantz** (Berlin), **Archive Books** (Berlin), **Distanz** (Berlin), **Phaidon**.

**Physical conventions.** European catalogue trim sizes cluster at 165 × 240 mm and 170 × 240 mm; A4 (210 × 297) for cheap production; 240 × 300 mm for photography. Bindings that signal art-book rather than trade-book: **Swiss brochure** (cover glued only along the rear edge so the spine is exposed and the book lies flat), otabind, exposed sewn spine, and singer-sewn for short runs. Typefaces that recur: Akzidenz-Grotesk, Times, Plantin, Arnhem (Fred Smeijers), Lyon (Kai Bernau), GT Sectra, Founders Grotesk, Neue Haas Grotesk.

### 3.4 The DIY visual language

**Risograph.** The RISO duplicator (RISO Kagaku; RZ, MZ, SF and RP series machines) prints **one spot colour per drum**, in sequential passes, using soy-based inks, at stencil-duplicator speed and cost. Named inks that define the look: **Fluorescent Pink**, **Federal Blue**, Bright Red, Yellow, Green, Teal, Aqua, Purple, Burgundy, Metallic Gold, Black. Fluorescent Pink in particular is unreproducible in CMYK, which is precisely why it reads as "not corporate."

The aesthetic rests on things a commercial printer would treat as defects: **misregistration** between passes, roller marks, ink transfer onto the following sheet, uneven coverage in solids, and coarse halftone dots. These are kept, not corrected. Riso is cheap at volume and poor at photographic fidelity, which makes it structurally suited to a space that has more time than money and more artists than budget.

Named presses: Knust / Extrapool (Nijmegen), Hato Press (London), Risotto Studio (Glasgow), Colour Code (Toronto), Perfectly Acceptable (Chicago), and in Berlin Drucken3000 and Bongoût.

**Photocopy and zine lineage.** Mimeograph, then the punk cut-and-paste of *Sniffin' Glue* (Mark Perry, 1976), Situationist *détournement*, riot grrrl zines, and the poster work of Gran Fury for ACT UP (*SILENCE = DEATH*, 1987). The visual markers: extreme contrast, halftone breakup from re-photocopying a photocopy, toner grain, visible tape and scissor edges, typewriter or Letraset type, a deliberate refusal of the smooth.

**Cheap production markers that read as honest rather than poor.** Folded A3 into a four-page A4 handout. Two staples, saddle stitch. Unbleached, recycled or coloured stock. One spot colour. Laser black. **No bleed**, because the office printer cannot do it, so the white margin becomes part of the language. Hand-numbering. A rubber stamp instead of a printed logo.

**Flyposting.** *Wildplakatieren* is formally illegal in most German cities and fined, but tolerated in practice on construction hoardings, and legal alternatives exist through culture-poster services and Litfaßsäulen. The layered, torn, over-pasted poster wall is itself the graphic identity of the off-space scene, and it is a genuinely public medium in a way no mailing list is.

**Free and open typefaces small spaces actually use:** Inter (Rasmus Andersson), Space Grotesk and Space Mono, Work Sans, IBM Plex, Redaction, the Velvetyne library (Karrik, Basteleur, Compagnon), Collletttivo, and Iosevka (Belleve Invis). Using an open font is not only a budget decision; it is legible as a position, which is why community-run spaces reach for them.

### 3.5 Bilingual and multilingual text

Six standard typographic strategies, with what each one says:

1. **Two columns side by side.** Primary language left. Egalitarian if the type is identical. Doubles panel width.
2. **Two weights.** Primary regular, secondary light or italic. Compact, but italic for a whole second language hurts readability and reads as a downgrade.
3. **Two sizes.** Secondary at 80 to 90 percent. Efficient and unambiguously hierarchical.
4. **Two colours.** Primary black, secondary mid-grey. Readable but the grey usually fails contrast requirements.
5. **Two separate panels**, one per language. Fully equal, doubles the wall area.
6. **Second language off the wall entirely**, onto the room sheet or a QR-linked page. This is what almost every small space does, because two-language vinyl roughly doubles installation cost.

**Same size and same weight, in two columns or two blocks, is the only genuinely equal option. Every other choice is a statement about whose room it is.**

Regional practice worth naming: Canada, where the Official Languages Act obliges federal institutions such as the National Gallery of Canada to give English and French equal prominence, which produces the strictest bilingual typography anywhere. Switzerland handles German, French and Italian. Belgium handles Dutch and French. MACBA in Barcelona runs Catalan, Spanish and English. Berlin institutions overwhelmingly set German first, English second, at equal size with a lighter weight or a hairline rule between, and put any third language on paper.

## 4. Small and community spaces

### 4.1 Berlin and Germany

**Named spaces.**

- **KW Institute for Contemporary Art**, Auguststrasse 69, Berlin Mitte. Founded 1991 by a group of young curators including Klaus Biesenbach in the ruin of a former margarine factory. It became the founding institution of the **Berlin Biennale** (first edition 1998). The canonical arc from squat to institution, and the reason Auguststrasse became the gallery street of 1990s Berlin.
- **Tacheles**, Oranienburger Strasse: squatted February 1990 in the ruin of a 1908 department store, running as studios, gallery, cinema and club until it was cleared in 2012.
- **ACUD / ACUD MACHT NEU**, Veteranenstrasse 21: gallery, cinema, studios, club and bar in one building, where the bar underwrites the art programme. The clearest demonstration of the bar-as-funding-model.
- **ZK/U, Zentrum für Kunst und Urbanistik**, Moabit: founded 2012 by the artist group KUNSTrePUBLIK in the disused Güterbahnhof Moabit; residencies plus public programme plus a garden and neighbourhood use.
- **SAVVY Contemporary**, founded 2009 by **Bonaventure Soh Bejeng Ndikung**, now in Wedding. Self-described as "the laboratory of form-ideas," explicitly built around the meeting of Western and non-Western knowledge production, with a kitchen, a bar and a library as load-bearing parts of the programme rather than amenities.
- **Kunstraum Kreuzberg / Bethanien**, in the former Bethanien hospital, run by the district: a municipal, free, non-commercial exhibition space.
- **Galerie Wedding**, inside the Rathaus Wedding: a district-run Kunstraum, free entry, publicly funded, deliberately embedded in a local government building rather than a gallery district.
- Peers and successors: District Berlin, Grüntaler9 (Wedding), After the Butcher (Lichtenberg), and a long tail of *Projekträume* that appear and close on two-year leases.

**Public support structure.** The Berlin Senate's **Auszeichnung für Projekträume und -initiativen** is an annual prize awarded to roughly twenty Berlin project spaces, each receiving a grant in the low tens of thousands of euros, administered by the Senate culture administration. It is unusual in funding the *space* rather than a project, which is what a small space actually needs. The **netzwerk freier berliner projektraeume und -initiativen** is the self-organised advocacy body, and its main campaign is commercial tenancy law (*Gewerbemietrecht*), because rising *Gewerbemiete* on short leases is the mechanism that kills off-spaces.

**The Kunstverein and the e.V.: the actual legal architecture of community ownership.**

Germany has on the order of 300 Kunstvereine, organised through the **ADKV (Arbeitsgemeinschaft Deutscher Kunstvereine)**. The oldest include Kunstverein Hamburg (1817), Kunstverein für die Rheinlande und Westfalen in Düsseldorf (1829), Kölnischer Kunstverein (1839) and Basler Kunstverein (1839).

The legal form is the **eingetragener Verein (e.V.)**:

- Requires at least seven founding members.
- Registered in the **Vereinsregister** at the local Amtsgericht.
- Governed by a **Satzung** (statutes) and a **Vorstand** (board) elected by the **Mitgliederversammlung** (general assembly), where **each member has one vote**.
- Applies to the Finanzamt for **Gemeinnützigkeit** (charitable status), which makes donations tax-deductible and exempts charitable activity from corporation tax.
- Members pay annual dues, typically in the range of tens of euros a year for an individual, reduced for students, higher for supporting members.

This is not a technicality. It is the concrete answer to "community-owned, no company, no gatekeeper." The e.V. is a legal person with no owner and no shareholders; its assets belong to its purpose, not to anyone; and its director is accountable to a membership that can vote them out.

**Jahresgaben** are the mechanism that makes it work financially. Each year the Kunstverein commissions or acquires limited editions from exhibiting artists and offers them to members at a low price, usually shown in a dedicated December exhibition. Members get an affordable original, the artist gets paid and gets distribution, and the Verein gets income and a reason for people to renew. It is the most elegant funding instrument in the German art world and it is directly transposable.

### 4.2 UK

- **Transmission Gallery**, Glasgow, founded 1983 by graduates of Glasgow School of Art. Its governance is the single most important anti-gatekeeping design in the artist-run world: an unpaid **committee of six who serve a fixed term (typically two to three years) and must then step down**, replacing themselves. Enforced rotation means no one can accumulate power in the space, and the programme is structurally forced to keep changing hands.
- **Cubitt**, London, founded 1991: artist studios plus a gallery plus a curatorial fellowship selected by the studio members.
- **Studio Voltaire**, London, founded 1994 in a former Methodist chapel in Clapham: subsidised artist studios underwriting a free public programme, plus **House of Voltaire**, an editions shop that fundraises by selling artist-made objects. Same logic as Jahresgaben, different wrapper.
- **Eastside Projects**, Birmingham, founded 2008 by Gavin Wade: describes itself as an "artist-run multiverse" and treats the gallery itself as an artwork under continuous construction, so the space is never finished and never neutral.
- **Auto Italia** (London, 2007), **Grand Union** (Birmingham), **Islington Mill** (Salford, 2000, Bill Campbell), **Catalyst Arts** (Belfast, on the Transmission rotating-committee model), **Generator Projects** (Dundee), **S1 Artspace** (Sheffield), **Spike Island** (Bristol).

### 4.3 US

- **112 Greene Street / White Columns**, New York, founded 1970 by Jeffrey Lew and Gordon Matta-Clark: New York's oldest alternative art space.
- **The Kitchen**, New York, 1971, founded by Steina and Woody Vasulka in the literal kitchen of the Mercer Arts Center.
- **Artists Space**, New York, 1972, founded by Trudie Grace and Irving Sandler with New York State Council on the Arts funding: the template for the publicly funded alternative space.
- **Printed Matter**, New York, 1976, founded by Sol LeWitt, Lucy Lippard and others as a non-profit for artists' books; now runs the NY Art Book Fair.

### 4.4 Welcome versus gatekeeping as a design problem

Every item below is a physical or procedural decision with a legible social meaning.

**The door.**

- The **buzzer and intercom** at street level is the strongest exclusion device in the entire vocabulary. It makes the visitor request permission, and be observed requesting it. Nothing else in a gallery filters as efficiently, and it costs nothing to install.
- A **propped-open door** converts entry from a request into a walk-in. In warm weather this alone changes footfall.
- **Glazing.** A transparent shopfront (*Ladenlokal*) lets you see what is inside before committing, which removes the fear of walking into the wrong thing. Frosted glass, blacked-out windows or a solid facade with a small nameplate require you to enter blind. Berlin off-spaces mostly keep clear shopfront glass. Blue-chip galleries mostly do not. That is not an accident.
- **Whether the name is on the door at all.** Some galleries have no sign. It is a filter, and it works.
- **The reception desk on axis with the entrance** means the first thing you meet is a person assessing you. Moving the desk to one side, to the back, or removing it entirely changes the first two seconds of the visit, which is where most people decide whether they belong.
- **The gallerist who does not look up.** The non-greeting is a class filter and it is cheaper than a doorman. The counter-design is a rule, not a feeling: greet everyone within five seconds, and say what the show is without being asked.

**Hours.**

- Commercial norm: **Tuesday to Saturday, 11:00 to 18:00**. That excludes anyone with standard working hours except on Saturday.
- Off-space norm: **Friday 18:00 to 21:00 and Saturday 14:00 to 18:00**, or "by appointment." "By appointment" is honest about resources and is simultaneously the strongest possible gate, because it requires the visitor to have a name to write to.
- Opening one evening a week after 19:00, or on a Sunday afternoon, is a statement about who you expect. So is closing in August.

**The vernissage.**

- German usage: **Vernissage** for the opening, **Finissage** for the closing. Berlin openings are conventionally **Friday from 19:00**.
- Social function: it is usually the only moment the space is full, the only moment the artist is present, and the only moment a visitor has a socially sanctioned reason to be there without buying anything. In Berlin the actual venue is the **pavement**, and the material basis is a crate of **Sternburg** ("Sterni") at well under a euro a bottle, a bottle opener and a donation box. A crate of beer costs less than a print run of invitation cards.
- The trap: a space whose audience only ever materialises at the opening has an audience problem disguised as a success. And a room full of people who all know each other is the most intimidating possible first visit for someone who does not. The design fix is a second, quieter event mid-run: a reading, a screening, a walkthrough with the artist at a time when nobody is drinking.

**Furniture of welcome.**

- **Seating.** Most galleries have none, and it is deliberate. O'Doherty's sentence is the diagnosis: "while eyes and minds are welcome, space-occupying bodies are not." A bench admits that visitors have bodies, get tired, come with children, and might want to stay. One bench changes a room more than any wall text.
- **The bar and the kitchen.** SAVVY Contemporary, ACUD and ZK/U all put drinking and eating at the centre. A bar gives a visitor something to do with their hands, a reason to stay after they have finished looking, and a revenue line that is not the art.
- **The reading table.** A shelf of books, zines and past room sheets gives a second reason to be in the room and a way to linger without performing contemplation.
- **The courtyard.** Berlin *Hinterhof* culture: a gallery with a courtyard is a different social object from a street-front one, because the courtyard is where the conversation happens.
- **The unglamorous list:** a working toilet, a kettle, somewhere to lock a bike, somewhere to put a pram, a coat hook, wifi, and step-free access.

**Money.**

- Free entry is the baseline for artist-run spaces and for most Kunstverein programmes. Ticketing is a gate, and the price is only part of it: the queue, the cloakroom, the timed slot and the bag check all add friction and all say "you are being processed."
- Publishing prices, or stating plainly that nothing is for sale, removes a specific anxiety: not knowing whether you have walked into a shop and are therefore a customer failing to buy.
- Sliding-scale membership and pay-what-you-can are the standard mitigations where a fee is unavoidable.

**The theory worth citing.**

- **Pierre Bourdieu and Alain Darbel, *L'Amour de l'art: les musées d'art européens et leur public*** (1966; English *The Love of Art*, 1990). Survey evidence that museum attendance tracks educational capital almost perfectly. The mechanism is the load-bearing insight for anyone designing a gallery: the museum's formal openness, free entry for all, conceals a **competence requirement** that is unequally distributed. The **absence of explanation is not neutrality, it is an examination** that only the already-educated pass. This is the argument that makes a wall label a political object rather than a service.
- **Nina Simon, *The Participatory Museum*** (2010) and ***The Art of Relevance*** (2016). Simon's door-and-key figure: relevance is not about how nice the room is, it is about whether the visitor is handed a key to a door they already wanted opened.
- **Museums Association (UK), *Museums Change Lives*** (2013, revised since): the social-impact framework that UK institutions are measured against.
- **Autry and Murawski, #MuseumsAreNotNeutral** (2017) and their *Panorama* essay (2019).
- **Carol Duncan, *Civilizing Rituals*** (1995) and **Tony Bennett, *The Birth of the Museum*** (1995).

### 4.5 Operating conventions of small spaces

| | Commercial gallery | Kunstverein | Off-space / Projektraum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Run length | 5 to 8 weeks | 8 to 12 weeks | 2 to 4 weeks, sometimes one weekend |
| Sales commission | 50 percent standard, 40 for established artists | Generally does not sell, except Jahresgaben | 0 to 30 percent, or nothing plus a donation box |
| Who pays production | Split, often gallery | Institution | Frequently the artist, which is itself a class filter |
| Artist fee | None, income is from sales | Increasingly standard | Rare, and increasingly a condition of Senate funding in Berlin |
| Text | Press release plus room sheet | Full interpretive apparatus and catalogue | One sheet, or the artist's own words |
| Governance | Owner | e.V. with voting members | Founders, informal, or a rotating committee |

**The typology.** The classic German off-space is the **Ladenlokal**: a ground-floor shop unit with a street-facing glass front, one or two rooms of 30 to 80 sq m, a back room, a sink and a toilet, direct street access, and cheap *Gewerbemiete* on a short lease. Its virtues as a gallery are structural: you can see in from the street, the door is at pavement level, there is no lobby and no staircase, and it sits among shops rather than in a cultural district, so passing by it is not a decision.

**Funding stack in Berlin:** the Senate Projektraum prize; the Hauptstadtkulturfonds; district (Bezirk) culture funds; Stiftung Kunstfonds; and, separately, *Kunst am Bau*, the German percent-for-art requirement on public construction budgets, which is a different pot but the largest source of public money reaching artists.

## 5. Principles worth keeping (physical)

1. **There is no neutral container.** O'Doherty: "we see not the art but the space first." Every default you inherit is an argument someone else made.
2. **The absence of explanation is an examination.** Bourdieu's mechanism. A room with no text is not open, it is closed to everyone who was not taught the code.
3. **Bodies before eyes.** The white cube welcomes eyes and minds and excludes bodies. A bench, a toilet, a kettle and a place to sit down are accessibility measures and they are also a politics.
4. **The gate is usually two metres from the door.** The buzzer, the desk on axis, the person who does not look up, and the price list you have to ask for. All four are cheap to install and cheap to remove.
5. **Room sheet over wall label** at small scale. Zero cost, zero wall damage, revisable to the last hour, and the visitor takes it home.
6. **Scale-appropriate apparatus.** A 50 sq m room does not need a title wall, an intro panel and section panels. It needs one line and one bench.
7. **Ownership has a legal form.** In Germany, "community-owned, no company, no gatekeeper" is the **e.V.**: seven founders, a Satzung, one member one vote, charitable status, dues, and Jahresgaben to fund it.
8. **Cheap production is legible as a position**, not just a budget. Riso misregistration, no bleed, two staples, an open-licence typeface. These read as "the money went to the artist," which is a claim about values, and it is only credible if it is true.

## 9. Artforum (artforum.com)

### 9.1 Status under Penske

Penske Media acquired Artforum in December 2022, having bought ARTnews and Art in America from Peter Brant in 2018. As of 2026 all three sit under Penske, with Artforum maintained as editorially independent. Co-editors **Rachel Wetzler and Daniel Wenger** took over in 2026. Footer reads: `Artforum is a part of Penske Media Corporation. © 2026 Artforum Media, LLC. All Rights Reserved.`

### 9.2 Typography

Served from Adobe Fonts, kit `kta7xdp`:

```
font-family: "franklin-gothic-urw",sans-serif
```

That is the **only** custom face. Everything else falls back to Penske's shared "nova" design system tokens: `var(--nova--font-family--arial)`, `var(--nova--font-family--helvetica)`, plus literal `font-family:Arial`. So Artforum's web presence is Franklin Gothic URW over an Arial/Helvetica chassis it shares with Rolling Stone and Variety. Given Artforum's print reputation, this is the biggest gap between print identity and web identity in this set.

Brand tokens exist but are thin: `--wp--preset--color--artforum-pink: #EA1989`, `artforum-dark-blue: #0E1A30`, `artforum-light-green: #C8F09B`, `artforum-light-gray: #F7F7F7`. Primary: `News` `Columns` `Features` `Previews` `Reviews` `Video` `Current Issue` `Archive` `artguide` `艺术论坛`

`Features` submenu: `SPOTLIGHTS`, `CLOSE-UPS`, `INTERVIEWS`, `1000 WORDS`, `PORTFOLIOS`, `ARTISTS' PROJECTS`, `DOSSIER`

The nav preserves the print magazine's rubric names, which is the strongest surviving piece of Artforum identity online. `Current Issue` and `Archive` as top-level items assert the issue as the organising unit, unlike Artsy's or Ocula's feeds.

### 9.5 Tombstone convention

Artforum keeps the strictest scholarly caption of the five, including fraction glyphs and the double-prime inch mark:

> `Marc Kokopeli, Times Square Alliance Playset (detail), 2025, digital video (color, sound, 9 minutes 15 seconds), LCD cabinet, LED screen, resin, fabric, mixed media, 36 5⁄8 × 60 5⁄8 × 31 1⁄2″. Photo: Graysc.`

Field order: **Artist, Title (italic), (detail), Year, medium with duration parenthetical, materials list, dimensions in inches with `″`, Photo: credit.** Imperial only, no metric conversion, which is the reverse of Frieze.

Diary/social captions use a different convention: role plus name, for example `Artist Kenneth Tam, Megan Mi-Ai Lee, Jacob Jackmauh, Pam Lins, novelist and Trisha Baga's partner Molly Dektar.` and `All photos: the author.`

Review index rows carry a distinctive four-part stack: **City / Headline / Deck / Venue / By Author**, for example `London` / `Bend Me, Shape Me: Franco Bellucci's knotty sculptures at a. SQUIRE` / `Bellucci's assemblages show reality under duress` / `a. SQUIRE` / `By Alex Bennett`.

News items carry timestamps to the minute: `July 30, 2026 4:37 pm`.

**Paywalled via Piano/Tinypass.** The CSS contains `#piano-paywall` and `.pmc-paywall`, with the classic fade-out gate:

```css
._footer_16ec2_1{position:sticky;bottom:0;z-index:var(--piano--paywall--z-index)}
```

Plus `Login` / `Account` / `Manage Account`, newsletter registration gates, and a named newsletter suite: `From the Archive`, `Must See`, `Video`, `In Print`, `Dispatch`, `ArtforumEDU`.

The most striking editorial artifact is the corporate bleed. The homepage carries a `From Our Partners` block (sponsored posts bylined `By ArtPhilly`, `By Lehmann Maupin`, `By Kiang Malingue`) and, below the reviews, a **Penske-network `Most Popular` module** whose top three entries on the day of capture were:

followed by a `You may also like` rail sourced from `rollingstone`. Artforum's own criticism sits directly above Comic-Con trailer coverage.

### 10.1 Typography: the only serif reading face in the set

Frieze abstracts fonts behind semantic aliases, then maps them to a **bespoke commissioned sans plus a licensed serif**:

```
@font-face{font-family:regFont;        src:url(/themes/frieze/src/fonts/rebrand/Frieze-Regular.woff2)}
@font-face{font-family:mediumFont;     src:url(/themes/frieze/src/fonts/rebrand/Frieze-Medium.woff2)}
@font-face{font-family:lightFont;      src:url(/themes/frieze/src/fonts/rebrand/Frieze-Light.woff2)}
@font-face{font-family:boldFont;       src:url(/themes/frieze/src/fonts/rebrand/Frieze-Bold.woff2)}
```

Measured values, root 16px:

| Element | Face | Size / leading | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Article H1 | `mediumFont` (Frieze-Medium) | 33px / 36.3px | w500, ls 0.495px (0.015em), leading 1.1 |
| Section kicker | `regFont` | 11px / 13.2px | UPPERCASE, ls 0.275px, `#808080` |
| Card headline | `regFont` | 18px / 21.6px | ls 0.27px |

| `READ MORE` | `regFont` | 10px / 10px | UPPERCASE, ls 0.25px, white on dark |

Two decisions stand out. First, **body text is set at weight 100** in a Light serif at 18px with 1.85 leading, giving a very pale, airy page. ```

Primary: `Magazine` `Fairs` `Spaces` `Watch &amp; Listen` `Membership` `VIP`, then `Sign In`, plus an `EN` / `KO` language toggle.

- `Magazine`: `Frieze Magazine`, `Subscribe`, `New Issue`, `Archive`, `Partner Content`, `On View`, `Frieze New Writers`, `Shop`, `Critic's Guides`
- `Fairs`: `Frieze Los Angeles`, `EXPO CHICAGO`, `Frieze New York`, `Frieze Seoul`, `The Armory Show`, `Frieze London &amp; Frieze Masters`, `Frieze Abu Dhabi`

Editorial genres: `Critic's Guides`, `Opinion`, `Exhibition Reviews`, `Roundtables`, `News`. Article cards render the genre twice in a breadcrumb form: `Exhibition Reviews / Exhibition Reviews / [headline]`.

Frieze is the only site here whose IA is built around **physical real estate and fairs first**, with the magazine as one of six peers. It is a fair business with a magazine attached, where e-flux is a mailing list with an archive attached.

### 10.5 Business model

**No paywall** (no Piano, no meter, no subscriber-gate markup). Revenue comes from fairs, `Membership`, `VIP`, print `Subscribe`, `Shop`, `Partner Content`, and `Event Space Hire`. `Sign In` exists but gates nothing editorial.

### 10.6 Technical and performance

- A full OneTrust consent modal blocks first paint with `We Care About Your Privacy` and `Manage Consent Preferences` categories `Strictly Necessary`, `Performance`, `Functional`, `Targeting`. Its own H2/H3 render in `-apple-system`, visibly outside the brand.
- Light mode only.

| Frieze | No paywall; monetised through fairs, membership, print, venue hire |

### 11.4 Homepage priorities and omissions

- **e-flux** prioritises a reverse-chronological, date-stamped list of institutional announcements. It deliberately omits: hero imagery, prices, personalisation, recommendation rails, infinite scroll, dark mode, and any editorial ranking.
- **Artsy** prioritises browsable commerce and price. It omits an institutional/archival frame entirely; there is no "issue," no chronology, no archive.
- **Ocula** prioritises curated previews and editorial with a directory of galleries beneath. It omits prices and, notably, any transaction path.
- **Artforum** prioritises the print rubrics and the current issue. It fails to omit its parent company's traffic modules.
- **Frieze** prioritises fairs and physical spaces alongside criticism. It omits any marketplace function.

### 11.5 Motion and scroll

None of the five uses scroll-jacking, parallax, or view-transition animation. e-flux is the only one that declares `prefers-reduced-motion`. Neither Artsy nor e-flux uses infinite scroll on its primary index; Artsy uses numbered pagination to page 100, e-flux uses day-grouped chronological batches. Ocula's editorial index uses relative timestamps, the only feed-like convention present.

### 11.6 Dark mode

**None of the five ships dark mode on the web.** Artsy is closest: `v3Dark` tokens shipped in `palette-tokens` v7.3.0 on 27 January 2026, but artsy.net contains no `prefers-color-scheme` rule and renders `#ffffff`. If a dark-capable reference is wanted, the token file is the artifact to read, not the site.

Worth noting against DESIGN.md §7, which forbids dark mode on ratata.gallery: **the entire commercial art-media field agrees**. Light-only is the field norm, not a limitation.

## 13. Note for a community-owned, text-first system

The closest digital precedent is **e-flux**, and specifically three moves that cost almost nothing to implement:

1. A single typeface in four faces.
2. An 18px root so the whole scale sits one step larger than default.
3. **Roman versus italic at one size carrying the entire semantic load** between institution, person, and work title.

Its Criticism index in particular (title, author, nothing else) is the extreme case worth studying.

The closest physical precedent is the **Kunstverein / e.V.** model: it is the legal and financial architecture that makes "community-owned, no company, no gatekeeper" a structure rather than a slogan, and **Jahresgaben** is the funding instrument that makes membership worth renewing.
